Part 2 Specify competing binary logistic regression

I expect all three predictors to be related to survival. First, I expect female passengers to be more likely to survive than male passengers. In 1912, people often followed the rule of “women and children first,” so women may have had better access to the lifeboats. Second, I expect passengers in higher ticket classes to be more likely to survive. First-class passengers may have had cabins closer to the lifeboats and may have received more help from the crew. Third, I expect younger passengers to be more likely to survive than older passengers, because children may also have been given priority during the evacuation.

Code 
mod0 <- glmmTMB(Survived ~ 1, data = titanic,       
                family = binomial(link = "logit"))                

mod1 <- glmmTMB(
  Survived ~ Sex,                                   
  data = titanic,                                   
  family = binomial(link = "logit"))

mod2 <- glmmTMB(
  Survived ~ Sex + Pclass,                         
  data = titanic,
  family = binomial(link = "logit"))

mod3 <- glmmTMB(
  Survived ~ Sex + Pclass + Age,                    
  data = titanic,
  family = binomial(link = "logit"))

In each model, the intercept is the log-odds of survival for the reference group, that is, when every predictor is at its reference category or equals zero. In mod1, the intercept is the log-odds of survival for female passengers, because female is the reference category for Sex. In mod2, the intercept becomes the log-odds of survival for female passengers in first class, because first class is the reference category for Pclass. In mod3, the intercept is the log-odds of survival for a female, first-class passenger who is 0 years old. Since no passenger in the data is exactly 0 years old, the mod3 intercept is not very meaningful on its own.

Part 3 Compare model performance

The full model (mod3) provides the best fit to the data, with the lowest AIC (657.28) and the lowest BIC (680.14) of the four models. Both indices penalize model complexity, so these values indicate that mod3 offers the best balance of fit and complexity. Both AIC and BIC decreased with each added predictor, although the decrease became smaller at each step: AIC fell by about 212 with the addition of sex, 74 with ticket class, and 23 with age. The likelihood-ratio tests showed that each addition significantly improved model fit (all ps < .001). Sex produced the largest improvement, χ²(1) = 213.82, followed by ticket class, χ²(2) = 78.27, and age, χ²(1) = 25.15. AIC, BIC, and the likelihood-ratio tests therefore support the same conclusion.

The diagnostics suggest that mod3 fits the data well. First, the DHARMa residuals showed no distributional misfit, with non-significant uniformity (p = .903), dispersion (p = .812), and outlier tests (p = .541). Second, all VIFs were below 1.5. Third, there was no sign of complete separation, because every cell contained both outcomes and fitted probabilities ranged from .017 to .976. Fourth, the 290 survivors provide 58 events per coefficient, above the rule of 10. The only potential problem is a slight U-shape in the residual-versus-predicted plot, although the combined quantile test was non-significant. This pattern may reflect a non-linear age effect or an unmodelled interaction. Thus, the direction and significance of the associations appear trustworthy, but the age odds ratio is best read as an average linear trend.

Fourth, the diagnostics showed no serious problems: the DHARMa tests were non-significant, collinearity was low, and there was no complete separation. Finally, mod3 is easy to interpret and directly tests all three of my predictions.

Holding ticket class and age constant, male passengers had lower log-odds of survival than female passengers (b = -2.52, 95% CI [-2.93, -2.12], p < .001). In other words, the odds of survival for male passengers were 0.08 times the odds for female passengers (OR 95% CI [0.05, 0.12]).

Holding sex and age constant, second-class passengers had lower log-odds of survival than first-class passengers (b = -1.31, 95% CI [-1.85, -0.76], p < .001), so their odds of survival were 0.27 times the odds of first-class passengers (OR 95% CI [0.16, 0.47]).

Third-class passengers had even lower log-odds of survival than first-class passengers (b = -2.58, 95% CI [-3.13, -2.03], p < .001), and their odds of survival were 0.08 times the odds of first-class passengers (OR 95% CI [0.04, 0.13]).

Finally, holding sex and ticket class constant, each one-year increase in age changed the log-odds of survival by -0.04 (95% CI [-0.05, -0.02], p < .001). This means that each additional year of age multiplied the odds of survival by 0.96 (OR 95% CI [0.95, 0.98]), which is about a 3.6% decrease in the odds per year.

In this analysis, I used binary logistic regression to examine whether sex, ticket class, and age were associated with surviving the Titanic crash among 714 passengers. I compared four nested models, starting with an intercept-only model and adding sex, ticket class, and age one at a time. The full model (mod3) had the lowest AIC and BIC, and each added predictor significantly improved model fit. I therefore selected mod3 as my preferred model. In this model, sex was the strongest predictor of survival. Holding ticket class and age constant, the odds of survival for male passengers were 0.08 times the odds for female passengers (95% CI [0.05, 0.12]). Ticket class also mattered. Compared with first-class passengers, the odds of survival were 0.27 times as high for second-class passengers (95% CI [0.16, 0.47]) and 0.08 times as high for third-class passengers (95% CI [0.04, 0.13]). Finally, each additional year of age multiplied the odds of survival by 0.96 (95% CI [0.95, 0.98]). Thus, the results support all three of my predictions: female, higher-class, and younger passengers were more likely to survive.

Part 6: Discuss your findings and limitations

The results suggest that sex was the most important predictor of survival, followed by ticket class and then age. Adding sex to the null model produced by far the largest improvement in fit, χ²(1) = 213.82, compared with χ²(2) = 78.27 for ticket class and χ²(1) = 25.15 for age. This pattern fits the “women and children first” rule, which gave women the clearest advantage in reaching the lifeboats. Ticket class also mattered a great deal, even after sex and age were held constant. This suggests that socioeconomic status shaped who survived, possibly because first-class cabins were closer to the lifeboats. Age had the smallest effect, but older passengers were still less likely to survive. Thus, survival on the Titanic seems to have depended mostly on who a passenger was (sex) and partly on what they could afford (ticket class).

This analysis has several limitations. First, the sample includes only the 714 passengers with complete age data, so the results may not represent all passengers on board. Second, the model treats age as a straight-line effect, but the slight U-shape in the residual plot suggests that the age effect may not be linear. For example, children may have had a clear advantage that a single slope cannot fully capture.

survival for female passengers, because female is the reference category
for Sex. In mod2, the intercept becomes the log-odds of survival for
female passengers in first class, because first class is the reference
category for Pclass. In mod3, the intercept is the log-odds of survival
for a female, first-class passenger who is 0 years old. Since no
passenger in the data is exactly 0 years old, the mod3 intercept is not
very meaningful on its own.

# Part 6: Discuss your findings and limitations

The results suggest that sex was the most important predictor of survival, followed by ticket class and then age. Adding sex to the null model produced by far the largest improvement in fit, χ²(1) = 213.82, compared with χ²(2) = 78.27 for ticket class and χ²(1) = 25.15 for age. This pattern fits the "women and children first" rule, which gave women the clearest advantage in reaching the lifeboats. Ticket class also mattered a great deal, even after sex and age were held constant. This suggests that socioeconomic status shaped who survived, possibly because first-class cabins were closer to the lifeboats. Age had the smallest effect, but older passengers were still less likely to survive. Thus, survival on the Titanic seems to have depended mostly on who a passenger was (sex) and partly on what they could afford (ticket class).

This analysis has several limitations. First, the sample includes only the 714 passengers with complete age data, so the results may not represent all passengers on board. Second, the model treats age as a straight-line effect, but the slight U-shape in the residual plot suggests that the age effect may not be linear. For example, children may have had a clear advantage that a single slope cannot fully capture.
